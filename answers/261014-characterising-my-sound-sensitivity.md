# Characterising my sound sensitivity: misophonia, hyperacusis, SOR, noise sensitivity, or ADHD?

> Not medical advice, and not a diagnosis. This is a literature summary written for a
> personal notebook by someone who is not a clinician. The "provisional reading" section
> applies published definitions to a self-description; it is an organising hypothesis, not
> an assessment. See the disclaimer in the [README](../README.md).

## Short answer

1. **Probably not one thing.** My three current triggers each fit a *different* construct
   best, and the constructs come from different literatures with different standing.
   "A mix" is the honest answer. Not a single label with exceptions.
2. **Background speech while concentrating** → the **irrelevant sound effect**, amplified
   in ADHD. This is an attention/cognition phenomenon, not a sound-*tolerance* condition.
   Already covered in
   [`260812-background-speech-and-focus.md`](260812-background-speech-and-focus.md);
   nothing here changes that answer.
3. **Construction drilling at home** → best described by **noise annoyance** (environmental
   psychology), with **predictability and perceived control** as the driving variables,
   possibly on top of trait **noise sensitivity**. It is a poor fit for misophonia: one
   published critique of the consensus definition argues environmental noise such as
   neighbours should be *excluded* from misophonia outright.
4. **My son's yelling and screaming** → the weakest fit for misophonia, despite the anger.
   Screams are acoustically built to be aversive to everybody. What I record is a
   *cumulative* load ending in irritability and anger, not an instant trigger-locked
   reaction. That pattern fits the **sensory over-responsivity (SOR) / sensory-load**
   description better than misophonia. Misophonia isn't ruled out, though: a test for it is below.
5. **Hyperacusis looks unlikely** but has not been measured. Nothing I have recorded involves
   pain or loudness as such. My hearing is normal, though normal hearing doesn't rule
   hyperacusis out. One loose thread is that I escalate to earplugs for TV *when it is loud*.
   A 14-item questionnaire can settle this cheaply.
6. **Phonophobia** (anticipatory *fear* of sound) does not fit anything recorded.
7. **What would actually discriminate** is a handful of self-tests (listed at the end) plus
   a few deliberate comparisons: quiet-irregular vs loud-regular drilling, announced vs
   unannounced work, and my son's *quiet* repetitive sounds vs his loud ones.

## 1. The candidates, and which framework each belongs to

Per this notebook's rule, each term is named with the framework it comes from. They are
**not** interchangeable, and several are not diagnoses at all
([`decreased-sound-tolerance-terminology.md`](../sources/decreased-sound-tolerance-terminology.md),
[`spd-diagnostic-status.md`](../sources/spd-diagnostic-status.md)).

| Construct | Home literature | Formal standing |
|---|---|---|
| Misophonia | Psychiatry / psychology / audiology (contested) | Not in DSM-5-TR or ICD-11. Consensus *definition* exists (2022), no diagnostic criteria agreed |
| Hyperacusis | Audiology | ICD-11 (reported as AB7Y; still unverified against the WHO browser, see terminology note) |
| Phonophobia | Audiology (fear of sound); separately, neurology (migraine symptom) | Term used inconsistently; no agreed criteria |
| Noise sensitivity | Environmental psychology / acoustics | A *trait* measured on a continuum; not a disorder |
| Noise annoyance | Environmental psychology / noise policy | A *response* to a specific exposure; not a disorder |
| Sensory over-responsivity (SOR) | Occupational therapy (Ayres / Dunn / Miller traditions) | No formal standing outside autism (DSM-5-TR B4) |
| ADHD distractibility / sensory gating | Psychiatry (A1h); experimental psychology | A1h is a DSM-5-TR criterion; "gating deficit" is a mechanism hypothesis |

## 2. Misophonia: what the consensus actually says

The reference definition is the 2022 Delphi consensus by an expert panel convened by the
Misophonia Research Fund
([Swedo et al. 2022, *Frontiers in Neuroscience*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8969743/)).
Statements needed ≥80% panel agreement. The core text, verbatim:

> Misophonia is a disorder of decreased tolerance to specific sounds or stimuli associated
> with such sounds. […] Misophonic responses do not seem to be elicited by the loudness of
> auditory stimuli, but rather by the specific pattern or meaning to an individual. Trigger
> stimuli are often repetitive and primarily, but not exclusively, include stimuli generated
> by another individual, especially those produced by the human body.

The definition also says:
- Oral sounds are the most-reported triggers, with nasal sounds (breathing, sniffing) close
  behind.
- The commonest emotions are **anger, irritation, disgust and anxiety**, sometimes rage.
- Triggers can raise autonomic arousal, such as muscle tension.
- Onset is typically in **childhood or early adolescence**.
- Misophonia can co-occur with hyperacusis and tinnitus.

The panel recommended that research *exclude* people with hyperacusis so the two are not
conflated.

What the definition did **not** reach consensus on matters as much: mechanism, prevalence,
course, whether it is psychiatric or medical, and the relationship to hearing disorders.

### Core features, compressed

- **Trigger-specific.** A particular sound (or its source) sets it off, not sound in general.
- **Pattern-based and usually human-made.** Repetitive bodily sounds from *other people*,
  above all eating, chewing, breathing and sniffing. In clinical series, every trigger was
  human-made, and patients were not distressed by the same sound when they made it
  themselves (reported in an American Academy of Audiology summary; secondary).
- **The emotion is anger or disgust, not pain.** This is what separates it from hyperacusis.
- **Immediate and trigger-locked.** The reaction fires on detection of the trigger, and
  people describe difficulty disengaging from it.

### The "loudness-independent" debate

The consensus hedges ("do not *seem* to be elicited by the loudness"), and the hedge is
deliberate:

- **For independence.** A representative German survey describes typical triggers as
  "usually not excessively loud"
  ([Pfeiffer et al. 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00127-024-02707-0)).
  Reviews describe physical properties (loudness, pitch, timbre) as unrelated to the
  intensity of the reaction. Meaning and source dominate.
- **Against a clean separation.** Misophonia and hyperacusis frequently co-occur in
  audiology clinics. A 2026 *American Journal of Audiology* study found that self-reported
  hyperacusis and hyperacusis measured by loudness discomfort level (LDL) disagreed often.
  So the instruments meant to separate the two do not always agree with each other.
  Tyler et al.'s (2014) "annoyance hyperacusis" subtype also overlaps the misophonia
  description.
- **The proposed fix.** Aazh's commentary on the consensus
  ([Aazh 2022, *Frontiers in Neuroscience*](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2022.1077097/full))
  argues a future definition should state an exclusion. The primary reaction must *not* be
  ear pain or discomfort from excessive loudness. The same commentary argues that
  **environmental noise sensitivity, for example neighbours or air conditioning, should be
  excluded from misophonia.**

The practical reading is that loudness is not *what defines* a misophonic trigger. That
doesn't mean a louder trigger can never feel worse. If loudness is the main variable,
though, you are probably looking at a different construct.

### Does a child's yelling, or construction noise, ever qualify?

- **Construction noise:** essentially no, on the current definition. It is not bodily, not
  usually from a specific person's body, and environmental. The Aazh commentary would
  exclude it explicitly. It belongs to the noise-annoyance literature (§4).
- **A child's yelling or screaming:** possible in principle but atypical. The definition says
  "primarily, but not exclusively" bodily sounds from another person, so a person-generated
  vocal sound is not excluded. "Kids crying/screaming" turns up in informal trigger polls
  among people with misophonia, at well below the rate of eating sounds; no peer-reviewed
  frequency for it was found. Against counting it:
  - Screams are **loud**, and screams are aversive to *everyone* by design (§5), so a strong
    reaction does not need an unusual trait to explain it.
  - Misophonia onset is typically in childhood. A reaction that began with becoming a
    parent does not fit that history, though adult onset is reported.
  
  The test that would point towards misophonia is whether my son's **quiet, repetitive**
  sounds (eating, sucking, mouth noises, a repeated syllable) also trigger instant anger.

### How common, and does it go with ADHD?

Prevalence depends on the instrument used:
- About 5% in a German household sample using the MQ severity threshold, or 5.9% on
  AMISOS-R
  ([Jakubovski et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9720274/)).
- 18% "significant misophonia" in a UK representative sample using the S-Five
  ([Vitoratou et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10032546/)).

The ADHD link is weak in the data so far. Of 575 confirmed patients at the Amsterdam UMC
clinic, 5% had comorbid ADHD, against 26% with obsessive-compulsive personality traits
([Jager et al. 2020, *PLoS ONE*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7159231/)). A
UK birth-cohort analysis (secondary report) found childhood anxiety and depression, but not
ADHD, more common in people later reporting misophonia. So ADHD does not make misophonia
the default reading.

## 3. Hyperacusis and phonophobia

**Hyperacusis.** The clinician Delphi consensus defines it as *"a reduced tolerance to
sound(s) that are perceived as normal to the majority of the population or were perceived
as normal to the person before their onset of hyperacusis"*
([Adams et al. 2021, *International Journal of Audiology*](https://research.aston.ac.uk/en/publications/a-delphi-survey-to-determine-a-definition-and-description-of-hype)).
Tyler et al. (2014) split it into four subtypes, which often overlap:
- **loudness** hyperacusis: everyday sounds feel too loud;
- **annoyance** hyperacusis;
- **fear** hyperacusis;
- **pain** hyperacusis, sometimes called *noxacusis*.

The driver is **level**. Sounds of everyday loudness are perceived as too loud,
uncomfortable or painful, and that is true whatever the source, whatever you are doing,
and whatever the sound means. Normal hearing thresholds do not rule it out.

**Phonophobia.** In audiology, Jastreboff and Hazell use the term for an *anticipatory
fear* of sound. The fear is typically that sound will cause harm or worsen another
condition, like tinnitus, and it leads to avoidance. Neurologists use the same word for the
sound intolerance of migraine, which is a separate thing. Usage is inconsistent across
papers (see
[`decreased-sound-tolerance-terminology.md`](../sources/decreased-sound-tolerance-terminology.md)).
Not wanting the drilling to resume, or not knowing when it will end, is closer to
*uncertainty and loss of control* (§4) than to fear of harm from sound.

## 4. Noise sensitivity and noise annoyance

These come from environmental psychology and are **not disorders**. They are the
constructs used in research on traffic, aircraft and construction noise, and they fit
"noise getting into my home" better than any clinical label.

**Noise sensitivity (trait).** This is a stable individual tendency to react more strongly
to noise. It is usually measured with the 21-item **Weinstein Noise Sensitivity Scale**
([Weinstein 1978, *Journal of Applied Psychology*](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.63.4.458))
or the 35-item **NoiSeQ**
([Schutte et al. 2007, *Noise & Health*](https://www.proquest.com/docview/203684934)).
Job (1999) noted it is not unitary. A 2022 review
([Welch et al., "What is Noise Sensitivity?", *Noise & Health*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9743308/))
argues against treating it as one simple trait. The same review notes that sounds which
break the *expected* acoustic range of a setting are tolerated worse than sounds that
belong there. That is directly relevant to noise intruding on a home.

**Noise annoyance (response).** This is the reaction to a specific exposure. It is
predicted only partly by decibels. Field surveys find that non-acoustic factors explain a
large share of annoyance once noise level is held constant:
- attitude to the source;
- trust in whoever is responsible;
- fear of the source;
- **perceived control**;
- **predictability**;
- coping capacity;
- noise sensitivity.

Kroesen and colleagues modelled aircraft-noise annoyance as a **psychological stress
response**, and identified mistrust and **control** as especially important
([Kroesen, Molin & van Wee 2008, *JASA*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18537376/)).

**Predictability and control: the classic experiments.** Glass and Singer's laboratory work
(*Urban Stress*, 1972; read here via secondary summaries and a later
[replication report](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADP003299.pdf)) found:
- Predictable (regularly spaced) and unpredictable (randomly timed) noise were rated
  **equally annoying** at the time.
- **Unpredictable noise produced larger after-effects**: more proofreading errors and lower
  frustration tolerance *after the noise had stopped*.
- **Soft unpredictable noise produced slightly more errors than loud predictable noise.**
- Participants who believed they *could* switch the noise off showed largely abolished
  after-effects, **even if they never used the switch.**

The point to take from this is that predictability and control can matter *more than
level*. Unpredictable noise leaves an after-effect that lasts beyond the noise itself.

## 5. Why screams are a special case

Screams carry **"roughness"**: amplitude modulation at roughly 30–150 Hz, against about
4–5 Hz in normal speech. In fMRI, rough sounds (screams and alarms) drove stronger amygdala
responses, and listeners rated rougher alarms as scarier
([Arnal et al. 2015, *Current Biology*](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:75898)).
The study used adult screams; infant screams were named as a follow-up question. A
separate literature finds infant crying reliably evokes irritation and arousal in carers,
with *higher-pitched* cries producing more physiological reactivity (abstract-level
reading only; not filed as a source).

So a strong aversive response to a toddler's screaming is the *expected* response of a
human auditory system. In the misophonia literature, a trigger is aversive *only to the
person with misophonia*. That cuts against reading this trigger as misophonic.

## 6. SOR and the ADHD-specific accounts

**Sensory over-responsivity (SOR)** comes from occupational therapy (see
[`spd-diagnostic-status.md`](../sources/spd-diagnostic-status.md)). It describes
over-response *across modalities*: sound, touch, temperature, smell. Its trait instruments
include the **Adolescent/Adult Sensory Profile** (Brown & Dunn), with its "sensory
sensitivity" and "sensation avoiding" quadrants, and the **SRQ** (Bar-Shalita). Adults
with ADHD score elevated on sensory sensitivity, with the auditory modality most affected
([`bijlenga-2017-sensory-profiles-adult-adhd.md`](../sources/bijlenga-2017-sensory-profiles-adult-adhd.md)).
They also report more sensory-gating problems
([`micoulaud-franchi-2015-sensory-gating-adhd.md`](../sources/micoulaud-franchi-2015-sensory-gating-adhd.md)).

"ADHD sensory gating" is a **mechanism hypothesis**, not a label. It could underlie either
the speech problem or SOR-type load, and the prior answer found the filtering-versus-control
question unresolved.

### Which would actually discriminate for me

- **HQ**: the cheapest way to rule hyperacusis in or out. If I score low, the TV-loudness
  detail is about intelligibility or annoyance, not loudness intolerance.
- **DMQ trigger checklist, or S-Five-T.** The useful information is *which* sounds I
  endorse, more than the severity score. Endorsing eating, breathing or sniffing triggers
  would be the main evidence for misophonia. Endorsing only "loud" or "environmental"
  categories would count against it.
- **MisoQuest**: a short severity check. Its items centre on the person-generated-sound
  core, so a low score is informative.
- **NoiSeQ** over WNSS for me, because its **habitation** and **work** subscales map onto
  triggers 2 and 1 respectively.
- **A/ASP**: the only one of these that tests the cross-modal pattern (heat, clothing,
  smell) that the sensory-load model implies. The trade-off is cost.
- The **IHS, AMISOS-R and MQ** would add little beyond these.

## 9. Provisional reading of my profile

**Not a diagnosis.** This maps my own recorded description
([`context/profile.md`](../context/profile.md)) against the definitions above. Each "fit"
is a hypothesis that the tests in §10 could overturn.

### Trigger 1: intelligible background speech while concentrating

- **Best fit:** irrelevant sound effect, ADHD-amplified (DSM-5-TR A1h distractibility).
- **Why:** strongly state-dependent (absent when relaxing), graded by intelligibility
  ([`hongisto-haapakangas-sti-performance.md`](../sources/hongisto-haapakangas-sti-performance.md)),
  no anger or disgust recorded, normal hearing.
- **Against the alternatives:** misophonia and hyperacusis are both aversive *whether or not
  I am working*. This one is not.
- **Open thread:** I reach for earplugs for TV *when it is loud*, which keeps a loudness
  component on the table. Plausibly, louder speech is simply more intelligible over other
  sound (speculation). The HQ would help here.

### Trigger 2: construction drilling at home

- **Best fit:** **noise annoyance** driven by **unpredictability and loss of control**,
  possibly on top of elevated trait **noise sensitivity**.
- **Why:**
  - My account centres on loss of control over my home, and on not knowing when it will
    end. That is close to the Glass and Singer variables, and to Kroesen's control factor.
  - The noise breaches the *expected* soundscape of home (Welch 2022).
  - It persists outside work, which separates it from trigger 1.
- **Against misophonia:** environmental, not bodily, not a specific person's sound. The
  Aazh commentary would exclude it explicitly.
- **Against hyperacusis:** no pain or loudness complaint recorded. "Gets through earplugs"
  is probably explained by sound and vibration carried through the building structure,
  which earplugs do not block well (speculation; not sourced here).
- **Tests that would distinguish:**
  - **Quiet-but-irregular vs loud-but-regular.** If intermittent tapping or quiet drilling
    with no schedule bothers me as much as loud drilling, the driver is unpredictability.
    If loud, steady, predictable noise bothers me as much as loud irregular noise, it is
    level.
  - **Announced vs unannounced.** If the same work is less distressing when I know it runs
    09:00–12:00 and ends Thursday, predictability and control is the variable. A notice
    from the building is the field version of Glass and Singer's switch.
  - **Same noise, not my space.** If identical drilling passed in the street, or in an
    office I do not own, barely registers, the "territory and control" reading is
    strengthened. If it is just as bad, trait noise sensitivity or loudness is.
  - **After-effects.** Glass and Singer predict worse irritability *after* unpredictable
    episodes end. That would match the carry-over the sensory-load model already records.

### Trigger 3: my son's yelling and screaming

- **Best fit:** a **normatively aversive alarm signal** (roughness; §5) feeding a
  **cumulative sensory-load / SOR-type** pattern, rather than misophonia.
- **Why:**
  - I describe a *daily stressor* that *adds to* a load ending in irritability and anger.
    It is cumulative and pooled with heat, clothing and smell, not an instant
    trigger-locked rage.
  - Screams are loud and rough by design.
  - Onset coincides with becoming a parent, not with childhood.
- **Why misophonia stays open:** the emotion I record (anger) is misophonia's signature
  emotion, and the source is a specific person.
- **Tests that would distinguish:**
  - **Quiet repetitive sounds.** Do my son's *quiet* repetitive sounds (eating, mouth noises,
    a repeated syllable, banging a spoon) provoke an immediate anger spike? Yes would point
    towards misophonia. No would point to level and roughness.
  - **Someone else's child.** Does another child screaming at a similar level bother me
    about as much? If yes, the response is to the signal. If it is specific to my son, that
    is more misophonia-like, or more about parenting stress (out of scope here).
  - **Load-dependence.** Is a scream at 09:00 after a good night much easier to bear than
    the same scream at 18:00 after drilling and heat? Strong dependence on load favours
    the SOR / load reading. Misophonic reactions are described as firing regardless of
    state.

### Cross-cutting

- The **sensory-load model** has the strongest family resemblance to SOR: cross-modal
  inputs pooling into one capacity, irritability, then anger on overflow, with carry-over
  across days. SOR has no formal diagnostic standing outside autism, so "described by the
  SOR literature" is the defensible phrasing, not "has SOR".
- The A/ASP *sensory sensitivity* and *sensation avoiding* quadrants are where that would
  show up, and where adults with ADHD tend to score high (Bijlenga 2017).
- Whether a pooled cross-modal load is a measured construct is still on the open list in
  [`scope.md`](../context/scope.md). This answer doesn't settle it.

## 10. The self-tests, in order

1. **Khalfa Hyperacusis Questionnaire.** About 5 minutes; rules hyperacusis in or out.
2. **MisoQuest.** 14 items; cut-off 61. Gives a quick severity reading on the
   person-generated-sound core.
3. **DMQ.** Free fillable PDF. Do at least the **trigger checklist** and **Symptom Scale**.
   Which categories I tick is the most informative single result in this list.
4. **NoiSeQ** (or WNSS if NoiSeQ items cannot be obtained). The trait measure; compare the
   habitation and work subscales.
5. **Adolescent/Adult Sensory Profile.** Commercial. The only cross-modal test of the
   load/SOR reading; do it if the first four leave the picture unclear.

Alongside the questionnaires, keep a short **event log** for two weeks with these fields:
- trigger;
- rough level (quiet, normal, loud);
- regular or irregular;
- announced or not;
- what I was doing (focused or relaxing);
- load already carried that day (0–10);
- emotion and its timing (instant or building);
- how long the after-effect lasted.

The comparisons in §9 come from this log, not from any questionnaire.

## What this answer did not establish

- The original Khalfa (2002) and Glass & Singer (1972) texts were **not** read. Their
  cut-offs and findings are from secondary sources.
- There is no peer-reviewed frequency for infant or child screaming as a misophonia
  trigger. The only figure found was an informal online poll, which is not evidence.
- No study was found that varies the predictability of *construction* noise specifically.
  The predictability evidence comes from lab noise and from aircraft- and traffic-noise
  surveys.
- Whether stimulant medication changes any of these is still open (see
  [`scope.md`](../context/scope.md)).
