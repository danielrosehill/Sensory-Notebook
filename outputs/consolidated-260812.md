# About this document

This is a consolidated export of the Sensory Notebook, a public repository that does two things at once and tries never to blur them: it records longform, sourced answers to research questions about sensory processing, and it records one person's own sensory profile and the interventions he has actually tried.

**Keeping those apart is the point.** Every entry below carries a banner saying which kind it is. Research summaries cite sources; personal experience does not need a citation and is never presented as a finding.

**This is not medical advice.** It is written by someone who is not a clinician. Describing what an intervention does is not the same as telling you to use it.

Sensory terminology is contested. Where a term belongs to a particular framework — DSM-5-TR, ICD-11, the occupational-therapy literature — the entries name the framework rather than using the terms interchangeably.

### The story

### What it felt like before there was a name for it

The thing I actually noticed was narrow and specific: when I am trying to focus on
something, background conversation destroys it. Not noise — conversation. People
talking near me, someone taking a phone call in the same room. Open-plan offices are
inherently very challenging for me, and for years that was just a fact about me with no
explanation attached.

The earliest version of it I can point to is other people's television while I was trying
to study. That was a real problem growing up, and it is not a childhood memory — it still
happens, and I still deal with it the same way, by closing doors and then reaching for
earplugs if it is loud enough. Whatever this is, it has been the same shape for a very
long time.

What made it hard to place is that it is not a general intolerance of sound. In the
same room, with the same people, at the same volume, if I am relaxing rather than
working, it does not bother me at all. That asymmetry is the whole shape of the thing,
and it is the part that made it feel like it could not be a "hearing" problem.

### What it got misattributed to

Hearing, first. I have had hearing testing done and my hearing came back completely
normal. Which closed one door without opening another — a normal test result told me
what it was not, and nothing about what it was.

Beyond that, I do not know whether it is a specific pathology of its own or simply part
of the overall ADHD picture. That is the open question this notebook started with.

### What changed, when

**Ten years in Jerusalem, across several apartments** — and every one of them has been
noisy in a different way. There is a pattern to how it happens: you view a place, it is
pretty quiet, you move in, and you find out you viewed it at a quiet time of day. That has
happened to us recurrently, which is its own small lesson about how little a viewing tells
you.

**Undated, during the previous apartment** — honking at night. It was audible in the
bedroom and audible while I was working. What made it different from the background-speech
problem was that it had nothing to do with concentration: it was the feeling of not having
control of my environment, of the place that is meant to be a safe space from the noise of
the city being invaded by the noise of the city. Not insanity, but extremely stressful, and
it got me looking for literally any solution under the sun.

**Around 2023, in response to the above** — the audiology appointment where I had custom
earplugs made, after trying Sony over-ear ANC headphones, ANC earbuds and Loops.

The part of this worth recording is not the product. It is that **I had struggled with this

had found them life-changing. The plugs themselves I ended up not wearing much once I had

### The comparison that made it click

I asked other people whether it bothers them, including my wife. What came back was not
"yes, a bit less" — it was a different relationship to the sound entirely. She described
being able to deliberately tune *in* and hear somebody speaking, as a thing she chooses
to do. Whereas I cannot tune it *out*.

That is a subtle difference and I think a vastly telling one. Same room, same sound,
same physical input — and the difference is not sensitivity, it is which direction the
control runs. For her the default is filtered and attending is the act. For me the
default is unfiltered and there is no act available.

If that generalises at all, it is a useful thing to be able to say out loud to
neurotypical people, because "background noise bothers me" gets heard as "I am fussy
about volume," which is not the claim.

## Why does background conversation only wreck my focus when I'm trying to focus?

*Asked 2026-08-12 · answered 2026-08-12 · confidence: medium · tags: auditory, adhd, attention, workplace*

### The question

##### Why does background conversation only wreck my focus when I'm trying to focus?

I have ADHD, and I've always had a specific kind of sensitivity to sound that can be
roughly described like this: when I'm working, trying to focus on something, I find that
background sound — background conversations in particular — really puts my focus across.
Open-office designs are inherently very challenging for me for that reason.

I don't know if this is a specific pathology, or a recognised part of the overall ADHD
picture.

I've had hearing testing done and organically my hearing is totally normal.

And strangely, it's very focused, very specific for me — by which I mean, if I'm relaxing
in a space I can hear people having phone calls and conversations and it doesn't bother
me at all. It's only when I'm trying to focus that it's hard.

I've asked other people, including my wife, about whether this bothers her at all. She
can actually tune *in* and hear somebody speak, whereas I can't tune it *out*. I think
that's a subtle but vastly telling difference in how maybe neurotypical and neurodiverse
people can experience the exact same sensory input from a totally different perspective.

So: how might this be defined? Is it pretty typical of ADHD, and what specific mechanism
drives it?

### The answer

##### Background speech and focus: what it's called, whether it's an ADHD thing, and what drives it

> Not medical advice. This is a literature summary written for a personal notebook, by
> someone who is not a clinician. See the disclaimer in the *README*.

##### Short answer

1. **It has a name, and the name is not a diagnosis.** The effect where task-irrelevant
   background speech degrades performance is called the **irrelevant speech effect**, or
   more accurately the **irrelevant sound effect (ISE)**. It is a robust laboratory
   phenomenon found in everyone, not a pathology.
2. **Yes, it is bigger in ADHD, and that is measured, not folklore.** Adults with ADHD
   take a larger hit from irrelevant sound than matched controls, and the size of the hit
   tracks self-rated inattention.
3. **The state-dependence is not strange — it is predicted.** The ISE attacks verbal
   serial short-term memory specifically. When you are not running a verbal-serial task,
   there is nothing for it to corrupt. Sitting in a room relaxing is not a verbal-serial
   task. Writing, reading, coding and holding a mental thread are.
4. **The trigger is speech structure, not loudness.** In the office-acoustics literature
   the performance decrement scales with **speech intelligibility (STI)**, not sound
   level. This is why noise can help while conversation destroys you, and it is not a
   contradiction.
5. **Your normal audiogram is exactly what all of this predicts.** Nothing in this account
   involves your ears.
6. **What is genuinely unsettled is the mechanism** — specifically whether the deficit is
   an early sensory-filtering failure or a late attentional-control failure. The evidence
   points both ways and the field has not resolved it.

---

##### 1. The phenomenon has a name

The **irrelevant sound effect** is the reliable impairment of immediate serial recall by
task-irrelevant sound that participants are explicitly instructed to ignore. It has three
properties that matter for your question.

**It is speech-preferring.** In the largest recent test —
*Leist, Lachmann & Klatte (2025)*,
137 children and 98 adults — narrative speech impaired verbal recall more than
environmental sounds (dog barking, bells, siren) did, and environmental sounds still beat
silence. Speech is the worst case, not merely one case.

**It is not about volume.** That study ran at **≈ 59 dB L<sub>Aeq</sub>** — ordinary
conversational level — and still produced a large effect (η²p = .197).

**It is graded by intelligibility.** The office-acoustics literature has quantified this.
*Haapakangas, Hongisto & Liebl (2020)*
systematically reviewed 11 experiments manipulating the **Speech Transmission Index** and
confirmed a dose–response curve: decline begins above **STI ≈ 0.21**, and the decrement
**exceeds 15% above STI 0.6**. Where speech is audible at all, its effect relates more to
intelligibility than to sound level.

That last point is the single most useful fact in this answer. The damaging quantity is
*how comprehensible the speech is*, not *how loud it is*.

##### 2. Is it a recognised part of the ADHD picture?

Yes, in two separate senses that are worth keeping apart, because they come from different
literatures and make different claims.

###### Sense one: distractibility is literally an ADHD diagnostic criterion

DSM-5-TR inattention criterion **A1h** reads: *"Is often easily distracted by extraneous
stimuli (for older adolescents and adults, may include unrelated thoughts)."* So the
symptom you are describing is not an add-on to ADHD, it is one of the nine inattention
items. The parenthetical was added for DSM-5 and makes explicit that the distracting
stimulus can be internal as well as external.

Note what the criterion is a claim about: **attention**, not perception. The DSM does not
credit ADHD with any sensory criterion. Autism does have one — hyper- or hyporeactivity to
sensory input, criterion B4 — and ADHD does not. See
*spd-diagnostic-status.md*.

###### Sense two: the effect has been measured, and it is larger in ADHD

*Pelletier et al. (2016)* is the
directly on-point study: 32 adults with ADHD and 32 controls, serial recall in quiet vs
with irrelevant sound they were told to ignore.

- The ADHD group recalled worse in **both** conditions — some of the gap is baseline
  short-term memory, not distraction.
- The **extra** decrement from irrelevant sound was **larger** in the ADHD group.
- Performance under irrelevant sound **correlated with self-reported attention problems**.
- Sustained attention looked intact — the sound penalty *shrank over the course of the
  task*.

Alongside that, a separate literature finds elevated sensory hypersensitivity in adult
ADHD generally, with the **auditory modality most affected**
(*Bijlenga et al. 2017*), and
elevated self-reported sensory-gating problems that correlate with the ASRS inattention
factor (*Micoulaud-Franchi et al. 2015*).
In that last study the ADHD group scored higher on the Sensory Gating Inventory than not
only healthy controls but also patients with schizophrenia, and the authors' phrasing is
almost a restatement of your complaint: attention "involuntarily drowned by many
irrelevant environmental stimuli."

**So: typical of ADHD? Yes.** Both as a diagnostic criterion and as a measured group
difference. What none of that establishes is that it is *only* ADHD, or that ADHD explains
its particular shape in your case.

##### 3. What it is *not* — and why ruling these out is informative

Getting the label wrong here is not pedantry; each wrong label points at a different
literature and a different set of interventions.

###### Not hyperacusis

Hyperacusis is discomfort or **pain** at sound levels most people tolerate, driven by
**loudness**, measurable as reduced loudness discomfort levels. It is present whether or
not you are doing anything. Yours is level-independent and vanishes when you stop working.
Wrong construct. (ICD-11 does list hyperacusis, under AB7Y — see
*decreased-sound-tolerance-terminology.md*.)

###### Not misophonia

Misophonia is an intense **emotional** reaction — anger, disgust — to **specific trigger
sounds**, characteristically body sounds, at any volume. It is stimulus-bound and
emotionally loaded. Your description contains no anger and no specific trigger, and the
trigger set is "any intelligible conversation," which is a category, not a trigger.
Misophonia is also **not in ICD-11 or DSM-5-TR** at all.

###### Not straightforwardly APD

"Normal audiogram plus trouble with speech and background sound" is the classic route to
an **auditory processing disorder** referral, and there are two reasons to be careful. The
first is that APD's validity as a distinct entity is heavily contested — no consensus
definition, no gold-standard test, and critics argue most APD test batteries are
effectively tests of language and attention. It overlaps so heavily with ADHD that the
standard tests cannot separate them, and the field is drifting toward the softer term
"listening difficulties." See
*apd-diagnostic-validity.md*.

The second reason is sharper and specific to you. **APD is difficulty extracting the
signal you want out of noise. Yours is difficulty discarding a signal you do not want
while doing something else.** Those are close to opposite failures. You have not reported
struggling to follow a conversation in a noisy bar. You have reported being unable to
*stop* following one. An APD battery is not built to detect that.

###### Not "sensory processing disorder"

SPD is not in DSM-5-TR and not in ICD; the American Academy of Pediatrics recommended
against using it as an independent diagnosis in 2012. The SOR literature can usefully
**describe** a sensory difference; it does not provide a thing to *have*.

##### 4. Candidate mechanisms

Five accounts are live. They are not mutually exclusive, and the honest position is that
the field has not decided between them. What follows is what each predicts, so you can see
which ones your experience actually discriminates.

###### (a) Interference-by-process — the phonological/serial-order account

Speech is **changing-state**: it carries automatic, obligatory order information. If your
current task also requires holding items in serial order — reading a sentence, composing
one, tracking a line of argument — the two order representations collide. This is
**pre-attentive and obligatory**. You cannot decide not to do it.

*Evidence:* Leist et al. found adult **spatial** serial recall completely immune while
verbal recall was disrupted. Domain-general accounts predict both should suffer. The
authors read the adult immunity as favouring **phonological-store interference**.

*Prediction:* effort does not help. Only removing or degrading the speech helps.

###### (b) Attentional capture

Background speech intermittently grabs attention away from the task. Distinct from (a) and
— per the **duplex-mechanism account** (Hughes et al. 2013) — **controllable by top-down
task engagement**, whereas interference-by-process is not.

*Evidence:* in Leist et al., children's spatial recall *was* disrupted, by speech and
non-speech equally, and the disruption disappeared by fourth grade. Read as attentional
capture waning as attentional control matures.

*Prediction:* effort and engagement do help, at least partly.

###### (c) Sensory gating failure

A pre-attentive filter that normally attenuates repeated irrelevant input. Indexed by
**P50 suppression** in a paired-click paradigm. Micoulaud-Franchi et al. report reduced
P50 suppression in adult ADHD, correlating with self-reported gating problems.

*Prediction:* the failure is early, sensory, and upstream of anything voluntary.

###### (d) Load theory — a late, cognitive-control failure

Lavie's account: perceptual capacity is limited and allocated involuntarily. High
perceptual load exhausts it on the task and starves distractors; low load leaves spare
capacity that spills onto whatever else is around.

*Forster et al. (2014)* found adults with
ADHD more distracted than controls — **but** that raising perceptual load reduced
distraction **equally well in both groups**. If the ADHD deficit were in early perceptual
selection, load should have helped the ADHD group differently. That it did not points the
deficit at **late selection / cognitive control**.

**Note the direct conflict with (c).** The gating literature says the failure is early and
sensory; the load-theory result says it is late and control-based. Both are in print, and
this notebook is not in a position to adjudicate. Flagging it rather than picking a side.

###### (e) Moderate Brain Arousal / stochastic resonance — why *noise* can help

*Söderlund, Sikström & Smart (2007)*:
white noise **improved** cognitive performance in children with ADHD and **impaired** it
in controls — a cross-over interaction. The MBA model attributes this to stochastic
resonance, with the optimum noise level modulated by dopamine, so lower-dopamine
individuals need more external noise to reach it.

**This resolves the apparent paradox** of "noise helps me, people talking wrecks me."
White noise is stationary and unstructured: no order cues, no phonology, nothing for
mechanisms (a) or (b) to bite on. It just raises the arousal floor. Speech is
non-stationary and structured, and the structure is what does the damage.

*Caveat, and it is a real one:* replications are mixed, and the stochastic-resonance
mechanism was directly challenged in a 2024 *Neuropsychologia* paper. The **contrast**
between structured speech and unstructured broadband noise is well supported by several
independent literatures; the **dopaminergic explanation** for the benefit is contested.
Lean on the first, not the second.

##### 5. Why only when you're trying to focus — the crux

This is the part of your question the literature answers best, and the answer is that your
observation is not strange at all. It falls out of the findings in two layers.

**Layer one — the trivial but real part.** "Distraction" is only defined relative to a
task. With no task there is no performance to degrade, so there is nothing to notice. This
is true but unsatisfying, because it would apply to anyone, and your point is that it does
*not* apply to everyone equally.

**Layer two — the substantive part.** The ISE is not a general "sound bothers me" effect.
It targets **verbal serial short-term memory**. Leist et al. is the clean demonstration:
adults doing a *spatial* serial task at 59 dB of Danish newsreader showed **no disruption
at all**, while adults doing a *verbal* task at the identical sound level were reliably
impaired.

Map that onto your two states:

| State | Verbal-serial load | Predicted ISE |
|---|---|---|
| Working — reading, writing, composing, holding a thread | High | Large |
| Relaxing in the same room | ~none | ~none |

Same room, same people, same volume, opposite outcome — **because the variable that
changed is not the sound, it is whether you are currently running the one cognitive
process that overheard speech corrupts.** Your "strangely, it's very specific" is the
literature's central finding, not an anomaly in it.

There is a third layer worth flagging, and it is where I am least confident.
*Sörqvist & Marsh (2015)* argue
that **concentration shields against distraction** — the more engaged you are, the less
background processing happens. Read naively that predicts the opposite of your experience.
The reconciliation I would offer is that the shield is a property of the **engaged state**,
not of the **attempt to become engaged**: the vulnerable window is exactly the effortful
not-yet-locked-on period, and if ADHD means spending more time in that window and less
time inside the shield, the phenomenology follows. **(speculation)** — this reconciliation
is mine, not a claim any of these papers makes.

##### 6. "She can tune in; I can't tune out"

Your framing is sharper than the literature's usual phrasing, and mostly it holds up.

**What is supported.** In typical listeners, selective attention *enhances* cortical
tracking of the attended talker and *suppresses* tracking of the ignored one — the
auditory cortex ends up representing the attended speech and not the ignored speech. The
default state is filtered, and attending is an operation performed on top of it. That is
exactly the asymmetry you described from the other side.

**What is inference, not evidence.** The natural extension — that in ADHD the *separation*
between attended and ignored streams is reduced, so the ignored stream stays represented —
is a plausible prediction and it is consistent with the gating findings. But I looked and
**did not find ADHD-specific neural speech-tracking studies** to support it. The
behavioural claim (ADHD shows a larger ISE, weaker self-reported gating, reduced P50
suppression) is supported. The neural-tracking bridge is **my extrapolation**. Treat it as
a hypothesis, and as a good candidate for a follow-up question.

**One caution on the comparison itself.** A single person's introspective report about
their own attention is not data, in either direction — introspective access to one's own
filtering is poor, and "I can tune in when I want" may describe the experience of a shield
working rather than a mechanism. The comparison is a good *illustration*; it is not
evidence. It is recorded as personal narrative in *context/story.md*,
not as a finding.

##### 7. What follows from this

Framed as implications of the findings, not as recommendations — those belong in
*recommendations/* once something has actually been tried.

- **Optimise intelligibility down, not volume down.** The STI dose–response means the
  target variable is comprehensibility. Absorption, screens, distance from talkers and
  masking sound all lower STI. Masking *raises* total sound level while *lowering* STI,
  which is why it works and why "just make it quieter" is optimising the wrong quantity.
- **Structured vs unstructured is the distinction that matters** when choosing what to put
  in your ears. The evidence separates broadband/stationary sound from speech-structured
  sound; it does not license any particular product claim.
- **Masking has a ceiling.** Spatial release from masking means individual voices can stay
  intelligible up to roughly four talkers, and in office simulations low masking levels
  made multi-talker environments *worse*.
- **Enclosure changes what coping is even possible.** In a 1,078-worker survey, open-plan
  occupants resorted to headphones; shared-office occupants could take breaks, change
  workspace, close a door. Annoyance was more likely above 20 occupants and without
  acoustic treatment. This is the empirical shape of "open-plan is inherently challenging."
- **Individual susceptibility is documented** (Ellermeier & Zimmer, 1997), which is the
  citable basis for treating this as an individual accommodation question rather than a
  general office-design preference.
- **If the dominant mechanism is (a), effort is not a lever.** Interference-by-process is
  not controllable by trying harder. If it is (b), engagement helps. Worth attending to
  which one your own experience looks like, since they imply completely different
  strategies.

##### 8. Confidence and what's still open

**High confidence:** the ISE exists, prefers speech, is graded by intelligibility not
loudness, and targets verbal serial memory. The state-dependence you described follows
directly from that.

**Medium confidence:** the effect is larger in ADHD. One good direct study (Pelletier,
n=64) plus converging evidence from adjacent measures. Not a large literature.

**Low confidence:** which mechanism dominates. The gating evidence (early/sensory) and the
load-theory evidence (late/control) genuinely conflict. Also low: the MBA/dopamine
explanation for why unstructured noise helps.

**Not established at all, flagged as speculation:** the engagement-window reconciliation in
§5, and the neural-tracking account in §6.

**Caveat on sourcing across the board:** with the exception of Leist et al. 2025 (open
access, read in full record), everything here was read at abstract or secondary-summary
level. Each file in *sources/* carries its own provenance note saying which.

###### Follow-up questions worth asking

- [ ] Does neural speech-tracking research show reduced attended/ignored separation in
      ADHD? (Directly tests §6.)
- [ ] Does stimulant medication change ISE magnitude? Secondary sources claim
      methylphenidate normalises some sensory hypersensitivities — unverified. This is
      also open question 3 in *context/scope.md*.
- [ ] Does the habituation Pelletier found (sound penalty shrinking over the task) match
      lived experience, or is that a lab artefact of short sessions?
- [ ] What is the actual measured STI of a typical open-plan desk, and what would it take
      to get one below 0.21?
- [ ] Is there evidence separating interference-by-process from attentional capture in
      ADHD specifically? That is the question that would decide whether effort is a lever.

## Is the right earplug attenuation for a screaming baby the same as for sleep?

*Asked 2026-08-12 · answered 2026-08-12 · confidence: medium · tags: auditory, hearing-protection, products, parenting, safety, israel*

### The answer

##### Parenting versus sleep: the advice holds, but not for the reason you were given

> Not medical advice, and not safety certification. This is a literature summary written
> for a personal notebook by someone who is not a clinician or an acoustician. See the
> disclaimer in the *README*.

##### Short answer

1. **The audiologist was right, and the reasoning is better than "reuse what you have".**
   For a normal-hearing listener, a hearing protector attenuates the signal *and* the
   background by roughly the same amount, so the **signal-to-noise ratio at the eardrum is
   preserved**. Earplugs do not selectively delete the baby. This is measured, not
   theoretical: across 80 listeners and seven warning signals, wearing hearing protection
   **improved** audibility for normal-hearing listeners.
2. **The two cases do differ — and sleep is the harder one.** Detecting a sound while awake
   and being woken by it are different thresholds, and the sleeping threshold is higher and
   individually unpredictable. So a plug you have validated against *sleep* is
   **conservative in the right direction** when used awake. The advice you were given errs
   safe.
3. **"Unsafe" cuts both ways, and the direction you weren't worried about is the real
   one.** **Overprotection** is a recognised occupational failure mode: too much
   attenuation degrades communication, causes missed signals, and — the self-defeating
   outcome — makes people take the protection off. The target is a **band, not a maximum**.
4. **The crying is loud enough to be worth protecting against.** Measured levels from
   crying children fall between **99 and 120 dB(A)**, and the authors of that study
   independently recommend earplugs for parents.
5. **The siren limb cannot be answered numerically.** The Israeli siren specification is
   not publicly published, in English or Hebrew. Anyone who gives you a dB figure for it is
   probably quoting a burglar alarm.

##### Why preserved signal-to-noise is the whole answer

The intuition behind the original worry — that blocking sound might cut you off from the
baby — imports a model where an earplug is a switch. It is not. It is a subtraction applied
to everything arriving at the ear.

If the cry is 100 dB and the room is 40 dB, a 20 dB plug gives you an 80 dB cry in a 20 dB
room. The **60 dB gap between them is unchanged**. What decides whether you detect the cry
is not the absolute level but whether it clears your **masked threshold** — audibility
against the background — and that relationship survives attenuation nearly intact for a
normal-hearing ear. *sources/hpd-warning-signal-audibility.md*

This is why the railway study found HPDs *improving* audibility for normal-hearing
listeners rather than degrading it. It also explains where the argument fails: for
**hearing-impaired** listeners it reverses, because the attenuated signal can drop below an
already-elevated absolute threshold. The authors propose a usable criterion — security is
assured where average absolute threshold at 500/1000/2000 Hz in the better ear stays
**below 30 dB HL**. Your hearing tested completely normal
(*context/profile.md*), which places you in the group the finding
applies to, not the exception.

##### Why sleep is the harder case, not the easier one

This is the part that actually answers "are these different requirements".

Being woken is governed by the **auditory arousal threshold**, which is not the same
quantity as your waking detection threshold — and there is **no correlation between the
two**. A normal audiogram tells you nothing about how easily you wake. Roughly 55–70 dBA at
the pillow wakes most adults, regulations generally specify **75 dBA at the pillow** for
bedroom alarms, and even so **20% of adults in one study slept through a ten-minute
alarm**. *sources/sleep-arousal-thresholds-and-alarms.md*

So the ordering is: **arousal threshold > waking detection threshold**. A plug specified so
that a baby can still wake you is, by construction, a plug through which you can hear a
baby while conscious. Going the other way — validating for parenting and assuming it covers
sleep — would be the unsafe direction, and is not what you were advised to do.

*(Speculation, flagged: nobody has measured arousal thresholds through earplugs. Subtracting
an attenuation figure from an arousal threshold is a reasonable inference and not a
finding. It is the weakest structural step in this answer.)*

##### The direction of "unsafe" you were not asking about

You framed the request as *take the edge off without being unsafe*, meaning: don't block so
much that I miss something. The occupational literature has a name for exactly that
concern — **overprotection** — and treats it as a real failure mode rather than a
theoretical one: missed safety signals, degraded communication, feeling "out of touch",
slower responses, and protectors being removed in order to hear.
*sources/hpd-overprotection-and-derating.md*

Two consequences:

- **In moderate noise, 10–15 dB of real attenuation is typically all that is needed**, and
  guidance frames the goal as a protected level in the **70–80 dBA** region — a floor as
  well as a ceiling.
- **Labelled attenuation is not obtained attenuation.** OSHA halves the NRR; NIOSH subtracts
  25% for muffs, 50% for foam plugs and **70% for all other earplugs** — the category custom
  moulds fall into. Whatever number is on the box, assume you are getting substantially
  less, which incidentally makes over-blocking less likely than the box suggests.

So the audiologist declining to sell you a heavier pair is consistent with the hygiene
literature, not just commercially decent.

##### The siren: what can and cannot be said

**Cannot:** there is no public specification for the sound-pressure level of the Home Front
Command siren network. Searched in English and Hebrew on 2026-08-12 across press, Hebrew
Wikipedia and civil-defence material, and it is not there. The 115 dB and 125 dB figures
that surface are **commercial burglar-alarm sounders** — a different product class
entirely. *sources/israeli-siren-level-not-published.md*

**Can, structurally:** this exact problem is what **ISO 7731** exists for. Its introduction
states that correctly designed danger signals reliably call attention to a hazard **even
when hearing protection is worn**, and its normative Annex B gives the method for
calculating the **effective masked threshold** — a quantity whose definition explicitly
folds in hearing-protector attenuation as a known input. The standard's position is that a
protected listener is a normal design case, not an excluded one.
*sources/iso-7731-auditory-danger-signals.md*

**Can, factually about the system:** the Israeli alert is **multi-channel by design** —
public sirens, the Home Front Command app, the national emergency portal, radio and TV
interruption, and location-based SMS with loud audio notification, staged from 15–30
minutes ahead down to roughly 90 seconds before impact. The siren is one path among
several, and its own documentation concedes coverage is incomplete in some areas. That is a
description of how the system is built, not a recommendation about what to rely on.

**The honest bottom line on this limb:** the general acoustic argument says a
normal-hearing person wearing moderate attenuation is not thereby deaf to a loud
low-frequency external siren. But that is reasoning from principle with **the key input
missing**, and it is about a *sleeping* listener, where the evidence is weakest. This
notebook cannot responsibly turn that into an assurance, and does not.

### The answer

##### Ideal attenuation for focused work: the number does not exist, and the question is aimed slightly wrong

> Not medical advice. A literature summary for a personal notebook, written by someone who
> is not a clinician or an acoustician. See the disclaimer in the *README*.

##### Short answer

1. **There is no published ideal attenuation figure for concentration.** Not "it is
   disputed" — it has not been established. No study found measured attenuation against
   cognitive performance. The confident numbers online come from earplug manufacturers.
2. **More is not better, and that part is well supported.** **Overprotection** is a
   recognised failure mode with a stated target *band* rather than a maximum. Hearing
   protection has an optimum.
3. **The variable that predicts performance loss is intelligibility, not level** — which
   means an earplug is a slightly indirect instrument for the problem in your profile.
4. **Whether it scales with severity is unstudied.** Every study found used unselected
   adults. The question is not contested; it is unasked.
5. **Yes, there is a cost to over-blocking** — increased listening effort, and one small
   study found worse mood and higher anxiety while occluded. But there is also an
   **acclimatisation effect**, so first impressions understate a plug.
6. **The flatness of the curve probably matters more than the number on the box.**

##### Why the number does not exist

The honest state of the evidence, and the reason this answer carries `confidence: low`
despite being fairly definite:

- Research on hearing-protection devices found **all tested HPDs degraded performance on
  most tasks** versus the open ear, specifically by **increasing listening effort** —
  cognitive resources spent on hearing are not available for the task. That is measured in
  conditions where you still need to monitor sound; in silent solo work it should largely
  not arise, but that boundary is reasoned, not tested.
- One pilot study — **thirty adults, two hours** — found visual attention **improved**
  during occlusion and stayed elevated afterwards, while **state anxiety rose and mood
  fell**. The same review notes prior studies were **inconsistent**, some finding nothing.
- Almost everything else asserting that earplugs aid concentration is **manufacturer
  content** — Loop, CURVD, Snugs, Hearprotek. It leans on the solid *noise impairs
  cognition* literature and then makes an untested leap to *therefore earplugs improve
  productivity*.

*sources/hpd-cognitive-performance.md*

This notebook carries a Loop recommendation. That recommendation stands on its own terms —
it records what happened when you used them. It is not evidence, and the company's
marketing is not evidence either, and keeping those apart is the point of the repository.

##### The question is aimed slightly wrong, and that is the useful finding

The whole quantitative apparatus for background speech and work performance is built on
**STI — the Speech Transmission Index**, not on decibels. What predicts performance loss is
**how intelligible** the speech is, not how loud. Performance decline begins around
**STI ≈ 0.21**, and where speech is audible at all its effect relates more to
intelligibility than to level.
*sources/hongisto-haapakangas-sti-performance.md*

ISO 3382-3 fixes two thresholds: **distraction distance** at STI 0.50 and **privacy
distance** at STI 0.20, where speech falls under 30% word intelligibility. The graded
experiments cluster low — a cut-off near 0.23, performance unaffected between 0.00 and
0.30, effort required to resist distraction from about 0.26 upward. So the target is
**around 0.2, not 0.5**.
*sources/iso-3382-3-distraction-and-privacy-distance.md*

**The catch: STI is a property of a room and a listening position, not of an earplug.**
There is no defined way to convert an attenuation figure into an STI change, so
"aim for STI 0.2, therefore buy an N dB plug" is a step the literature does not license.

This is why your own profile note reads the way it does. If intelligibility rather than
volume is the operative variable, then a plug — which lowers level roughly uniformly and
leaves the speech/background ratio intact — is attacking the problem side-on. The levers
that actually move STI are **absorption, screens, distance, and masking sound**, and
masking *raises* total level while *lowering* intelligibility. Your instinct to close a door
(*recommendations/closing-doors.md*) is, in these
terms, a better-targeted intervention than the earplugs are.

*(Speculation, flagged: this reasoning also predicts that an earplug should help less with
the speech-while-concentrating trigger than with the honking-at-night one — which is
exactly the distinction your profile draws for independent reasons. Suggestive, not
demonstrated, and the notebook does not currently record whether the plugs help with the
speech case at all.)*

##### Is more blocking better? No, and here is the shape of the ceiling

**Overprotection** is a documented occupational failure mode: degraded communication,
missed signals, feeling "out of touch", slower response, and protectors removed so people
can hear. In moderate noise **10–15 dB of real attenuation is typically sufficient**, with
target protected levels quoted in the **70–80 dBA** region — a floor as well as a ceiling.
*sources/hpd-overprotection-and-derating.md*

Against that, **labelled attenuation is not obtained attenuation**. NIOSH derates
non-foam earplugs by **70%**. So the practical risk of accidentally over-blocking with an
off-the-shelf number is lower than the box implies — and the practical value of a *fitted*
plug is that its delivered attenuation is more predictable, not that it is higher.

##### Does the answer differ if you are more affected?

**Unstudied.** Every study located used unselected adults; none stratified by sensory
over-responsivity, and none looked at ADHD populations.

What is documented is that **individual susceptibility to the irrelevant speech effect
varies**, and that variation is measured, not anecdotal — already catalogued in the
existing STI source. That is the empirical hook for saying a population-average acoustic
target under-protects some people. It supports *"the right target is not the same for
everyone"*. It does not tell you which way, or by how much, and it is about room acoustics
rather than hearing protection.

So the second limb of the question is a genuine open research question, and worth stating
as one rather than answering thinly.

##### Is there a point where blocking too much is its own problem?

Three real effects, none quantified against a threshold:

- **Listening effort**, above — a cost paid whenever you still need to hear something.
- **Mood and anxiety**, from the thirty-person pilot. One small study; recorded because it
  is the only signal found in either direction, not because it is solid.
- **Flatness of the curve.** The steeper the attenuation slope across frequency, the more
  the protected ear differs from the open ear. Germany's IFA codes protectors for this:
  **code W** requires a mean slope no greater than **3.6 dB/octave**, **code X** an
  extremely flat **≤ 2 dB/octave**. Foam is the bad case — it kills highs far harder than
  lows, which is why everything sounds muffled through it.
  *sources/hpd-warning-signal-audibility.md*

**Practical reading: choose flat over strong.** A 15 dB flat filter and a 25 dB steep plug
are not two points on one scale; the first leaves the world sounding like itself, quieter.
Filters around **15 dB are reported to be the flattest**, and a solid unfiltered custom plug
is **25–30 dB** — the blunt end of the range.
*sources/custom-earplug-attenuation-and-filters.md*

##### The finding that vindicates how this notebook already chose

Every recommendation in this repository was decided on **comfort**, with no attenuation
figure compared — noted in
*recommendations/README.md* before any of this research was
done. Two results say that was not a lazy proxy:

- **Overprotection's characteristic outcome is people removing the protection.** Wearability
  is not a soft criterion; it is the one the safety literature keeps arriving at.
- **Acclimatisation is documented**: pianists' reported degradation dropped between a first
  and second performance. So judging a plug on first wear systematically underrates it —
  which is worth knowing before rejecting one, and is the closest thing to actionable advice
  this answer contains.

##### What would close the gap

- **Which filter, if any, is in the custom plugs.** Still unrecorded, still the single most
  useful missing fact.
- **Any study measuring attenuation against cognitive performance.** None found; if it
  exists, it answers the question as posed.
- **Anything stratifying acoustic targets by sensory over-responsivity.** Would answer limb
  two, which is currently unanswerable rather than merely unanswered.

- **They are a mitigation, not control of your environment.** They made the honking
  survivable. They did not give me back the thing that had actually been taken, which was a
  bedroom that was quiet because it was quiet.
- **Whether they help with the primary auditory trigger is not recorded.** The profile's
  main problem is intelligible background speech while concentrating, which is a filtering
  problem rather than a loudness problem. Whether these plugs do anything for it, in an
  open-plan office or otherwise, is simply not something this file knows.
  

#### Transferability

For someone else to get the same result:

- **You need an audiologist who takes ear impressions and makes custom moulds.** This is not
  a mail-order product; the fit is the entire value.
- **The gain is comfort, not attenuation.** If off-the-shelf plugs already sit comfortably
  in your ears and you wear them, custom moulds buy you much less. The before/after here is
  "unwearable" to "wearable, all day, by choice" — not "quiet" to "quieter". If you have
  ever abandoned earplugs because they felt dry and intrusive rather than because they
  didn't block enough, this is the specific thing custom moulds fix.
- **Ask for material by use case, not one pair for everything.** Soft silicone for sleeping
  and lying on your side; hard acrylic for daytime. Buying one pair to cover both is the
  mistake this avoided.
- **Buy spares if you can afford to.** Ideally I would have a spare of each material. In
  practice I have one spare acrylic pair. These are small objects that live in a cabinet
  and get carried around; losing the only one at the wrong moment is the obvious failure
  mode.
- **Cost was ~USD 100 for the set of two**, and will vary a lot by country and provider.
  Treat it as an order of magnitude. Worth knowing that the price is what deferred this for
  years, and in hindsight the deferral was the expensive part.
- **Judge the provider by whether they will talk you out of a purchase.** The most useful
  thing mine did was tell me I did not need to buy anything else. A provider who sells you a
  third pair for every new situation is telling you something.

Earmuffs worn **on top of** earplugs, rather than instead of them — the doubling-up

## Loop earplugs

*kind: product · verdict: partial · since: 2023-00 · cost: unknown · modality: auditory*

#### Loop earplugs

Personal experience, not evidence. Not medical advice — see the disclaimer in the
*README*.

#### The problem

Same problem as the custom moulds: honking at night in the previous Jerusalem apartment,
and a search for anything that would help. Full background in
*custom-moulded-earplugs.md*.

#### What was tried

Loop earplugs, bought before the custom moulded pair.

There was a second reason I came to them, separate from the noise. **Loop have done
genuinely important work normalising sensory difficulties.** When they became popular, I
started hearing from friends — people I would never have associated with sensory issues at
all — that Loops had been life-changing for them. That surfacing effect is real and I have
a lot of respect for it. The product got people talking about something they had been
handling silently.

#### What happened

**They work, and I like them.** This is not a negative review.

**But the custom moulds were significantly more comfortable, and comfort decides which pair
you actually wear.** The customs are the ones I have on all the time; the Loops are not.
Once I had both, the choice made itself.

#### What it did not fix

- **Comfort, relative to a custom mould.** This is the entire gap. An earplug that is
  merely tolerable loses to one that is inert, every time, because the decision gets made
  fresh every day.
- **Nothing about attenuation is being claimed here.** I have not compared what the two
  actually block. The comparison I am making is about wearability, and it should not be
  read as a performance comparison. 

#### Transferability

- **Start here rather than at the customs, if you have not tried anything.** They are far
  cheaper, they need no appointment, and for a lot of people — including friends of mine —
  they are the end of the search rather than a step in it.
- **The reason to escalate to custom moulds is comfort, not performance.** If Loops sit
  fine in your ears and you wear them, you have already got the result and can stop.
- **If you abandon them, notice why.** "Didn't block enough" and "I couldn't stand having
  them in" point at different next purchases.
