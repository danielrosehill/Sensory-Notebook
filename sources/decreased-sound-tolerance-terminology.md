# Decreased sound tolerance: hyperacusis, misophonia, phonophobia — and what none of them are

Terminology hygiene note. Its job is to stop the notebook reaching for a
sound-tolerance label for something that is not a sound-tolerance problem.

**Decreased Sound Tolerance (DST)** is the umbrella term for elevated sensitivity to
sound in forms that would not bother most people. It co-occurs with autism, ADHD,
anxiety disorders and OCD; an estimated 50–70% of autistic people show clinically
significant DST at some point.

## Hyperacusis

Physical discomfort or **pain** when any sound reaches a level of loudness that would be
tolerable to most people. Exaggerated loudness perception with aversive reactions to
moderate-to-loud sound. On pure tones, patients judge tones uncomfortable at lower
loudness levels than controls (loudness discomfort levels, LDLs).

**Diagnostic standing:** ICD-11 classifies hyperacusis under AB7Y, "Other specified
disorders of ear, not elsewhere classified," in Chapter 10 (diseases of the ear or
mastoid process). Phonophobia appears separately as MB42, "Hypersensitivity to sounds,"
in Chapter 21 — which in fact corresponds more closely to the clinical description of
hyperacusis. ICD-10 defined it as a disproportionate increase in the sensation of
loudness at normal volume; ICD-10-CM H93.233 for bilateral.

**Assessment:** despite estimated prevalence of 10–15%, there is **no objective clinical
test**. Assessment relies on interview, questionnaires and LDLs, which lack reliability
and ecological validity. Consensus definitions were only developed recently (Delphi
consensus), which previously made prevalence estimates unreliable.

## Misophonia

Intense **emotional** reaction — anger, extreme irritation, disgust, anxiety — to
specific trigger sounds, characteristically body sounds (chewing, tapping, sniffling),
**even when those sounds are not loud**. Can evoke fight-or-flight symptoms: muscle
tension, raised heart rate, sweating.

**Diagnostic standing:** **not listed in ICD-11.** Not in DSM-5-TR either.

## The discriminating questions

| | Hyperacusis | Misophonia | Irrelevant sound effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Driven by | Sound **level** | Specific **trigger identity** | Signal **structure / intelligibility** |
| Emotional valence | Discomfort/pain | Anger, disgust | Usually none — just failure |
| Present when not doing a task? | Yes | Yes | **No** |
| Graded by intelligibility? | No | No | **Yes** |
| Formal standing | ICD-11 AB7Y | None | Not a disorder — a lab effect |

The row that does the work is **"present when not doing a task."** Both hyperacusis and
misophonia are properties of the stimulus and are aversive whether or not you are busy. A
sound problem that vanishes when you stop working is not a sound-tolerance condition, and
labelling it as one points at the wrong literature and the wrong interventions.

## Note on "normal audiogram"

The sources surfaced did not directly address DST occurring with clinically normal
pure-tone thresholds, though it is implied by the observation that hyperacusis is
frequently associated with tinnitus and peripheral hearing loss in the general
population, whereas in autism it is thought to be neurodevelopmental. Do not treat "my
audiogram is normal" as ruling anything in or out here.
