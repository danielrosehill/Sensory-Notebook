# Sensory processing disorder and sensory over-responsivity: what has standing and what doesn't

## Status of the labels

| Term | Standing |
|---|---|
| **Sensory Processing Disorder (SPD)** | **Not in DSM-5-TR. Not in ICD.** No specific ICD-10 code; clinicians use F84.9 or F88. |
| **Sensory Modulation Disorder / Sensory Over-Responsivity (SOR)** | Occupational-therapy constructs; SOR is framed as a subtype of SMD. No formal diagnostic standing. |
| **Hyper- or hyporeactivity to sensory input** | **Has standing — but only inside autism.** Folded into the revised ASD criteria in DSM-5 (criterion B4, alongside unusual interest in sensory aspects of the environment). |
| **"Easily distracted by extraneous stimuli"** | **Has standing — inside ADHD.** DSM-5-TR inattention criterion A1h. |

## Origins

The construct comes from occupational therapist and psychologist **A. Jean Ayres** in the
1970s: difficulty detecting, modulating and interpreting sensory stimuli to the point of
impairing daily functioning, with heightened or reduced emotional, behavioural or
physiological responses to stimuli of normal intensity.

## The criticisms

1. **Non-specificity.** In 2012 the **American Academy of Pediatrics** issued a policy
   statement recommending paediatricians **not use SPD as an independent diagnosis**,
   advising them to consider ASD, ADHD or anxiety disorder instead.
2. **Discriminant validity.** Ambiguity in the symptoms — they characterise several
   diagnoses — means even neuroimaging does little to settle the disagreement. Many
   children identified with sensory issues as preschoolers are later diagnosed with ADHD
   or anxiety; some are never diagnosed with any DSM disorder.
3. **Scope of the dispute is narrower than it looks.** There is general agreement that
   some children have serious sensory challenges. The disagreement is over whether SPD
   should be an independent disorder, how far sensory issues explain behaviours, and
   whether sensory-based OT treatment affects functioning.
4. **Bipolarity is questionable.** Hyper- and hypo-responsivity are conventionally treated
   as opposite poles. A 2025 medRxiv preprint ("The Sensory Paradox,"
   doi:10.1101/2025.09.21.25336283) reports a *positive* association between them, which
   complicates the standard subtype model — and is consistent with
   [`bijlenga-2017-sensory-profiles-adult-adhd.md`](bijlenga-2017-sensory-profiles-adult-adhd.md)
   finding adults with ADHD elevated on **both**.

## Evidence advanced by proponents

A diffusion tensor imaging study of 16 boys reported abnormal white matter microstructure
— the first quantifiable regional brain-structure difference between boys with SPD and
typically developing boys. A follow-up examined 40 children with SPD and 41 without,
including girls, screening out ASD using the Social Communication Questionnaire. Sample
sizes are small; treat accordingly.

## Prevalence figures in circulation

- SPD among US children: **5–16%** — a range wide enough to indicate the construct is not
  measuring one thing consistently.
- Sensory over-responsivity symptoms among children with ADHD: **46–69%** across studies.

Both should be quoted with the ranges attached, never as point estimates.

## Bearing on this notebook

Per [`CLAUDE.md`](../CLAUDE.md), the notebook names the framework a term belongs to rather
than using them interchangeably. Practical consequence: it is defensible to say a
sensory-processing difference is **described** by the SOR literature, and not defensible
to say someone **has SPD** as though that were a diagnosis, because in DSM-5-TR and ICD
terms there is nothing to have.
