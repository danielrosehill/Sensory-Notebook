# How concentration shields against distraction

A theoretical review, not an experiment. Included because it is the piece of the
literature that speaks most directly to the *state-dependence* question — why the same
sound is a problem in one state and not another.

## Core claim

Higher concentration makes you **less** susceptible to distraction, for two reasons:
undesired processing of the background environment is reduced, and the locus of
attention becomes more steadfast.

"Concentration" here is not a trait. It is **task-engagement**: the deliberate attempt to
compensate for high task difficulty. As the task gets harder, you engage more to hold
performance, and a side effect of that engagement is that peripheral processing is
suppressed. The authors offer as an everyday example the failure to notice someone
calling your own name.

## Supporting structure

- Task demands vary as perceptual load, sensory load, and cognitive load.
- **Visual**-perceptual load suppresses processing of distractors in the **auditory**
  modality too — the shield is cross-modal.
- The shield covers internally generated distractors (task-unrelated thought) as well as
  external ones.
- High working-memory-capacity individuals are typically less distracted by unwanted
  sound, show more attenuation of background processing when task difficulty is high,
  and show more resistance to attentional capture.

## Empirical companions

- Halin, Marsh, Haga, Holmgren & Sörqvist (2014), *JEP: Applied* — increased task demands
  producing greater focal-task engagement shielded against background speech during
  proofreading.
- Hughes, Hurlstone, Marsh, Vachon & Jones (2013) — **duplex-mechanism account**:
  auditory **attentional capture** is controllable via greater top-down task engagement;
  **interference-by-process** is not.

That Hughes distinction is the load-bearing one for this notebook. It means "try harder
to concentrate" is a coherent strategy against one of the two mechanisms and useless
against the other.

## The apparent contradiction, and why it isn't one

Read naively, this literature predicts that trying to focus should *protect* against
background speech, which is the opposite of the experience recorded in
[`context/profile.md`](../context/profile.md). The reconciliation is that the shield is a
property of the **engaged** state, not of the **attempt to become engaged**. The window
in which you are trying to focus and have not yet locked on is exactly the low-engagement
window in which the shield is down. This reconciliation is *my* reading and is not a
claim the paper makes — treat it as speculation.
