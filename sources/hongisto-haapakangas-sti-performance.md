# Speech intelligibility (STI), not loudness, predicts performance loss

The quantitative backbone for anything this notebook says about open-plan offices.

## The metric

**Speech Transmission Index (STI)** — Steeneken & Houtgast (1980) — is an objective,
measurable 0–1 index of how intelligible speech is at a listening position. It is the
standard descriptor in room acoustics, which is why the office-design literature is built
on it rather than on dB.

## The model

Hongisto (2005) proposed a dose–response curve relating cognitive performance to STI. It
was influential — it shaped office acoustic design guidelines — but rested on scarce data.
Haapakangas, Hongisto & Liebl (2020) revised it via systematic review of laboratory
experiments that manipulated STI, identifying **11 experiments** in which short-term
memory task performance was tested at differing STI values.

## Findings

- Strong experimental evidence that **performance decreases as STI increases**. The 2005
  hypothetical model is confirmed and revised.
- **Decline begins above roughly STI ≈ 0.21.**
- **Performance decrement exceeds 15% at STI above 0.6.**
- Where speech is audible at all, its effect on performance relates **more to
  intelligibility than to sound level**.

## Why this is the single most actionable finding in the set

It inverts the intuitive remedy. The variable to attack is **intelligibility**, not
volume. Turning speech down helps only insofar as it reduces STI, and there are cheaper
and more effective levers on STI: absorption, screens, speaker–listener distance, and
**masking sound** — which *raises* the total sound level while *lowering* STI. Anyone
whose instinct is "make it quieter" is optimising the wrong quantity.

The 2020 paper frames this economically: because reducing STI raises measurable work
performance, the payback time of room-acoustic investment can be calculated.

## Nuances

- The effect is not strictly about speech. What drives it is the **non-stationary**
  character of the signal in amplitude and frequency; non-speech signals with speech-like
  characteristics disrupt too. This is why the field renamed it the irrelevant **sound**
  effect. In an office, though, the non-stationary signals are overwhelmingly speech, so
  intelligibility indicators remain the right predictor in practice.
- Competing metrics exist: Schlittmeier et al. (2008) proposed **fluctuation strength**;
  a newer short-term temporal-modulation metric performs comparably to STI or FS while
  being easier to measure in situ.
- **Masking is not a free win.** Babble from multiple talkers masks, but *spatial release
  from masking* means individual speech can stay intelligible up to roughly four talkers.
  In realistic office simulation, low broadband masking levels meant multi-talker
  environments degraded performance *more*.
- **Individual differences are documented, not anecdotal:** Ellermeier & Zimmer (1997),
  *JASA*, on individual susceptibility to the irrelevant speech effect. This is the
  standard empirical hook for arguing that population-average acoustic targets
  under-protect some workers — i.e. the citable basis for an individual accommodation
  request rather than a general office-design complaint.

## Field evidence

A cross-sectional survey of 1,078 workers (IJERPH 2019, PMC6351961) found irrelevant
speech increased annoyance and reported performance loss and well-being symptoms more in
open-plan than in shared 2–5 person offices. Annoyance was more likely with **more than 20
occupants** and in offices **lacking acoustic treatment**. Open-plan workers resorted to
headphones with music; shared-office workers took breaks, changed workspace, closed the
door, or worked from home — i.e. small enclosed rooms enable coping strategies that open
plans foreclose.
