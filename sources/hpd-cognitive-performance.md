# Does wearing earplugs help you concentrate? The evidence is thinner than the marketing

The file that exists mainly to stop the obvious claim being over-read.

## What the peer-reviewed work actually found

**A cost: listening effort.** Research on hearing-protection devices found that **all
tested HPDs degraded performance on most tasks** compared with the open ear, and
specifically **increased cognitive load / listening effort**, measured by visual reaction
time in a dual-task paradigm adding working-memory demand. The framing: the extra effort
needed to perceive and process auditory signals through an HPD **consumes cognitive
resources** that other tasks would otherwise have.

That cost applies where you still need to monitor sound. In quiet solo work with nothing
to listen for, it should largely not arise — but that boundary is reasoned, not measured.

**A benefit, from one small study.** Thirty healthy adults wore earplugs in both ears for
two hours in a simulated-hearing-deficit pilot. **Visual attention performance improved
during occlusion and stayed elevated after removal** — while **state anxiety rose and mood
fell**. The same summary notes that **prior studies found inconsistent results**, some
showing no significant cognitive change.

**Task-specific interference, with acclimatisation.** Pianists performing with hearing
protection reported altered coloration, difficulty gauging dynamics and articulation, and
increased effort — **more pronounced with foam than with musician earplugs**. An
**acclimatisation effect** appeared: the degradation on the first performance decreased by
the second. That is the one finding here with a direct practical implication — judging a
plug on first use will underrate it.

## The marketing caveat

Most "earplugs improve concentration" material online comes from **earplug manufacturers**
— Loop, CURVD, Snugs, Hearprotek — not from independent research. Their claims rest on the
well-supported *noise impairs cognition* literature, but **the step from there to "earplugs
therefore boost productivity" is largely untested**.

This matters for a notebook that carries a Loop recommendation. The reason to record it is
not scepticism about the product; it is that the product's own marketing is not evidence,
and this repository is supposed to be able to tell the difference.

## What this source does not support

- **There is no good office-specific evidence** that hearing protection improves focused
  work. The honest summary is: plausible, mechanistically reasonable, largely undemonstrated.
- **No study here measured attenuation against performance.** Nothing supports "more
  attenuation means better concentration", which is the specific claim the notebook wanted
  tested.
- The mood and anxiety finding is from **one pilot study of thirty people** over two hours
  and should not be generalised. It is recorded because it is the only signal found in
  either direction on the affective cost of occlusion, not because it is solid.
- Nothing addresses **people with sensory over-responsivity specifically**. Every study
  here used unselected adults, so the question of whether any of this differs by severity —
  the second limb of the question that prompted this file — is **simply unstudied** in the
  material found.

## Provenance

**Relayed from search-result summaries.** No primary paper was opened; PMIDs and PMC
identifiers in the citation field come from search listings and **have not been verified to
match the claims attributed to them**. Author attribution for the situational-awareness
study is uncertain and should be checked before citation.

Given that provenance, this file is best treated as a **map of where the evidence is thin**
rather than as a source of findings. Its most reliable content is the negative claim — that
the office-focus literature does not exist in the form one would want — which is supported
by the absence of anything better across the whole search rather than by any single paper.
