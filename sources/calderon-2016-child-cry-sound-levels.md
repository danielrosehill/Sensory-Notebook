# How loud a crying child actually is at a carer's ear

The only measured figure this notebook has for the parenting case.

## Design

Sound intensity from **26 paediatric patients** was measured under two conditions: one
imitating the exposure of an examining physician, the other resembling that of a parental
guardian.

## Findings

- **All recorded levels fell between 99 and 120 dB(A).**
- The greatest risk of intense, potentially harmful cries was in children aged
  **9 months to 6 years**.
- The authors describe these levels as capable of causing **acute discomfort and mild
  pain**, with a theoretical risk that chronic exposure contributes to noise-induced
  hearing loss in a guardian or physician.

## The authors' own recommendation

Worth recording because it is unusually direct, and because it is the paper's actual
conclusion rather than an inference from it: the authors suggest **social workers and
medical personnel should consider recommending earplugs to parental guardians** of
frequently crying children. Their framing is that the stress of the sound can precipitate
impulsive reactions, and they position hearing protection partly as a child-protection
measure.

So the question "is it reasonable to wear earplugs around a crying child" has a published
answer, and the answer is yes. That is a much narrower claim than "here is the right
attenuation", which this paper does not address at all.

## What this source does not support

- **It is not an infant-specific figure.** The sample is paediatric patients across a
  range up to 6 years, in a clinical setting, and the highest-risk band starts at 9
  months. Reading it as "a newborn at 3 a.m. produces 120 dB(A)" over-reads it.
- **It gives no exposure duration**, so it cannot be turned into a dose. A 99–120 dB(A)
  peak and a sustained 99–120 dB(A) L_eq are very different exposures, and the abstract
  does not say which these are.
- **It says nothing about what level of attenuation is appropriate**, nor about the
  trade-off with still being able to hear the child. It establishes that the sound is
  loud enough to be worth protecting against. Everything after that is a different
  question.
- The exact separation between the two measurement conditions — physician-at-ear versus
  guardian — is not quantified in the abstract, so the "at a carer's ear" framing in this
  file's title is approximate.
