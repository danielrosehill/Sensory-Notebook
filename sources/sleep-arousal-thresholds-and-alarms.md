# Waking up is a different threshold from hearing

Synthesised note. The reason "can I hear it" and "will it wake me" are not the same
question, and the reason the sleep case is the harder one.

## Levels

- Roughly **70 dBA** is often cited as needed to wake most adults, though **55–60 dBA** can
  wake some when ambient noise is low.
- Sleep-laboratory work (Bruck & Horasan, 1995) puts arousal for unimpaired adults at
  **55–60 dB at 2000–4000 Hz, measured at the pillow** — but **20% of adult subjects
  stayed asleep at least once** through an alarm sounding for ten minutes.
- Regulatory requirements for alarm level **inside a bedroom are generally 75 dBA at the
  pillow** (USA, Canada, Australia), with an explicit caution that this may still not wake
  everyone.

## Frequency matters more than level

This is the finding that reframes the problem. The conventional high-pitched (3000+ Hz)
smoke-alarm signal is **significantly less effective than a 520 Hz square wave** in every
population tested.

- In hard-of-hearing adults, the 520 Hz square wave woke **92%** at 75 dBA against **56%**
  for the high-pitched alarm at the same level.
- In adults aged 65–85, the median arousal threshold for the most effective signal (mixed
  frequency, 500–2500 Hz) was **20 dBA lower** than for the standard US high-frequency
  signal. With high-pitched tones, many did not wake **even at 95 dBA at the pillow**.
- Strobe lights woke only **27%** of hard-of-hearing adults, above the US standard
  intensity.

Twenty dBA is an enormous margin — far larger than the difference between most hearing
protectors. It means signal design dominates attenuation in deciding whether a sleeping
person wakes.

## Individual variation is real and stable

There is a wide spread of arousal thresholds even after accounting for age and sleep stage.
An individual's threshold is **highly reliable night to night**, and someone with a high
threshold in one sleep stage has a high one in others. Critically, **there is no
correlation between waking and sleeping auditory thresholds** — you cannot predict how
easily you will wake from how well you hear.

Moderators: age, sleep deprivation, hearing loss, time of night, sleep stage, sex,
depression, sleeping tablets, alcohol, cannabis. Background noise of 44–53 dBA measurably
reduced responsiveness to 55 dBA alarms.

## Waking is not responding

When woken by a first alarm, **95% took no action within two minutes** and **92% did not
correctly interpret** what the signal meant.

## What this source does not support

- **None of this is about hearing protection.** No cited study measured arousal thresholds
  through earplugs. Combining the two — subtracting an attenuation figure from an arousal
  threshold — is an inference, not a finding, and is marked as speculation wherever this
  notebook does it.
- It is **fire-alarm literature**. Applying it to an air-raid siren assumes the two are
  comparable as acoustic signals, which is plausible but untested here.
- The "no correlation between waking and sleeping thresholds" point means a normal
  audiogram tells you nothing about your personal arousal threshold. Nobody's individual
  figure is knowable without measuring it.
